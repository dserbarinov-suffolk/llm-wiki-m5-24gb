The Antikythera mechanism is an ancient Greek hand-powered orrery, often described as the oldest known analog computer. It was retrieved from the sea in 1901 from a shipwreck off the coast of the Greek island of Antikythera and is dated to roughly 150–100 BC.

This complex device contains at least 30 bronze gears housed in a wooden case about the size of a shoebox. Users could turn a crank to move a pointer across a dial that displayed the positions of the Sun and Moon in the zodiac, the phase of the Moon, and the dates of solar and lunar eclipses based on the Saros cycle. Another dial tracked the four-year cycle of the Olympiad and other Panhellenic games.

Its level of sophistication was not surpassed in the surviving historical record until the astronomical clocks of medieval Europe, over a millennium later. The precision of its gear-cutting implies an established workshop tradition, although no comparable device or workshop has been discovered.

Derek de Solla Price conducted the first detailed scientific study in 1974, using X-ray imaging to count the gear teeth. The Antikythera Mechanism Research Project, starting in 2005, utilized microfocus X-ray computed tomography to read inscriptions inside the corroded fragments — effectively a user manual engraved on the device's plates. These inscriptions suggest the device may have originated in Corinth or its colonies, possibly connecting it to the school of Archimedes in Syracuse.
