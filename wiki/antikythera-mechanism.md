The Antikythera mechanism, an ancient Greek hand-powered orrery, is often described as the oldest known analog computer. It was discovered in 1901 from a shipwreck near the Greek island of Antikythera and is dated to approximately 150–100 BC.

This device contains at least 30 bronze gears housed in a wooden case about the size of a shoebox. By turning a hand crank, users could track the positions of the Sun and Moon in the zodiac, the phase of the Moon, and the dates of solar and lunar eclipses based on the Saros cycle. A separate dial monitored the four-year cycle of the Olympiad and other Panhellenic games.

The mechanism's sophistication was not rivaled in historical records until medieval European astronomical clocks, over a millennium later. Its gear-cutting precision suggests a level of technological advancement that may have been lost to history.
