The Antikythera mechanism is an ancient Greek hand-powered orrery, often considered the oldest known analog computer. It was discovered in 1901 from a shipwreck near the island of Antikythera and is dated to approximately 150–100 BC.

This device includes at least 30 bronze gears housed in a wooden case, roughly the size of a shoebox. By turning a crank, the user could track the positions of the Sun and Moon in the zodiac, the phase of the Moon, and the dates of solar and lunar eclipses based on the Saros cycle. A separate dial also tracked the four-year Olympiad cycle and other Panhellenic games.

Its level of sophistication was not rivaled in historical records until the astronomical clocks of medieval Europe, over a millennium later. The precision of its gears indicates an established workshop tradition, although no similar devices or workshops have been found.

Derek de Solla Price conducted the first detailed study in 1974, using X-ray imaging to count the gear teeth. The Antikythera Mechanism Research Project, starting in 2005, used microfocus X-ray computed tomography to read inscriptions hidden in the corroded fragments — effectively providing a user's manual on the device's plates. These inscriptions suggest the device may have originated in Corinth or its colonies and could be linked to the school of Archimedes in Syracuse.
